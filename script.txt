Why Don't Airplanes Fall Out of the Sky?

Right now, thousands of people are flying through the sky inside giant metal machines.

Some of these planes weigh hundreds of tons.

So why don't they simply fall?

And here's the surprising part: it's not the engines that hold them up.

To understand why, imagine sticking your hand out of a moving car window.

Tilt it slightly, and you'll feel the air pushing your hand upward.

An airplane wing works on a similar principle, but on a much bigger scale.

As the airplane moves forward, air flows around its wings.

Their shape and angle change how the air moves, creating a difference in pressure.

Typically, the pressure above the wing is lower than the pressure below it.

This helps create an upward force called lift.

But there's another way to understand what's happening.

The wing pushes a huge amount of air downward.

And according to Newton's third law, when the wing pushes air down, the air pushes the wing up.

These are two ways of describing the same physical process.

But there's a catch.

For the wings to produce enough lift, air needs to flow past them at sufficient speed.

That's why airplanes accelerate along the runway before taking off.

So what are the engines actually doing?

They create thrust, which helps the airplane overcome air resistance and maintain speed.

And now, the most surprising question.

What happens if both engines suddenly stop?

Does the airplane fall?

Not immediately.

It can keep gliding, using altitude to maintain forward speed while the wings continue producing lift.

That's right. A passenger jet can temporarily fly without engine power.

So next time you see an airplane overhead, remember:

It's not magic holding hundreds of tons in the sky.

It's invisible air, working with the laws of physics.
